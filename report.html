Reproducing the deep multi-criteria collaborative filtering model

Nassar, Jafar & Rahhal, A novel deep multi-criteria collaborative filtering model for recommendation system, Knowledge-Based Systems 187 (2020) 104811, doi:10.1016/j.knosys.2019.06.019.

This page covers the problem the paper set out to solve and how the model works. It then describes how it was rebuilt from scratch on the same dataset, what came out, and which of the paper's claims hold up.

1 · Problem statement

Why rate a hotel on seven things instead of one?

Most recommender systems learn from one number: the star rating a user gave an item. That number hides why the user liked it. Two people can both give a hotel 4 stars, one for the location and the other for the service. TripAdvisor asks reviewers for an overall rating and for seven criteria: value, rooms, location, cleanliness, check-in/front desk, service and business service.

The paper asks whether a neural network can use those extra criteria to predict the overall rating better than single-rating methods can. It follows the classic aggregation-function approach (Adomavicius & Kwon, 2007) in three steps:

  1. Predict the criteria. For a user and a hotel they haven't rated, predict all seven criteria ratings.
  2. Learn how criteria combine. Learn a function f with overall = f(value, rooms, …, business).
  3. Predict the overall rating. Feed the predicted criteria through f, then rank hotels for the user by that predicted overall rating.

The paper's contribution is to use a deep neural network for both step 1 and step 2. It reports beating SVD, SVD++, SlopeOne, baseline estimates, KNN-with-baseline and a single end-to-end neural network on every metric.

The task, in one line

Rebuild the model exactly as described in Sections 3 and 4.3, train and evaluate it the paper's way on the same TripAdvisor data, and report how our numbers compare with Tables 1, 2, 5 and 6.

2 · The model

Two networks, trained apart, chained at prediction time

The two networks never see each other during training. Net 1 learns to predict the seven true criteria from a user and a hotel. Net 2 learns to map the true criteria to the true overall rating. Only at prediction time does net 1's output feed net 2.

3 · The data

81,052 reviews, almost all from one-time reviewers

The data is Hongning Wang's LARA TripAdvisor crawl: one text file per hotel, each holding blocks tagged <Author>, <Content>, <Date> and <Rating>. The rating line holds 8 tab-separated numbers, with -1 meaning "not given". The parser splits on the tags rather than on blank lines, so stray carriage returns inside review text don't break it.

4 · Evaluation protocol

25 train/test runs and six metrics

Following the paper: 5-fold cross-validation, repeated 5 times with different shuffles, for 25 runs in all. Each run trains on 80% of the ratings, of which 1% is held out to decide when to stop training, and tests on the other 20%. Every number reported here is the mean ± sample standard deviation over those 25 runs. All seven methods use exactly the same splits.

Error

MAE: the average gap between predicted and true overall stars. Lower is better.

Top-K quality (K = 10)

F1 / F2: precision and recall of each user's top-10 test hotels; F2 weights recall more. A hotel is relevant when its true overall rating is 4 or 5.

Ordering

FCP: for each user's pairs of test hotels, the share that the model puts in the right order. Ties count against the model.

Ranking

MAP: how high relevant hotels sit in the top 10. MRR: 1 / rank of the first relevant hotel.

The paper leaves three things unstated

It doesn't give K, the relevance cut-off, or what counts as "recommended". We used K = 10 and relevant = 4+ stars, both adjustable flags. For "recommended", the published numbers only make sense one way. They are consistent with the surprise library's precision/recall recipe from 2019: a hotel counts as recommended when it is in the top 10 and its predicted rating is at least 4. With that definition our baselines reproduce the paper's F1 and F2 almost exactly, which confirms it.

5 · Work done

What was built

FileWhat it does
data.pyTag-based parser, the four filters with row counts, ID indexing, writes data/ratings.parquet, prints Tables 1 and 2 against the paper
model.pyCriteria DNN, overall DNN and Single DNN in Keras 3, exactly as in §3 and §4.3
metrics.pyMAE, F1/F2@K, FCP (eq. 14–18), MAP@K, MRR@K, computed per user over that user's test hotels
train.pyRepeated CV, early stopping, five surprise baselines, per-fold JSON plus saved predictions, resume, tables, ablation sweeps
run.shRuns training under caffeinate -i nice -n 10 so the Mac stays awake and responsive; logs to results/train.log
tests/Parser test on a synthetic 3-review file (a −1 rating, an anonymous author, a stray \r), plus checks of the FCP and ranking maths

Order of work

  1. Data first. Parsed and filtered the data and checked Tables 1 and 2 against the paper before training anything. Users and hotels match exactly.
  2. Smoke test. One fold, 2 epochs, end to end in about 20 seconds. Each baseline was then run once on a fold, which showed SVD matching the paper's MAE to the 4th decimal.
  3. Full run. 25 runs × 7 methods in 22 minutes on an M2 CPU.
  4. Found a failure mode. In one fold our model scored MAE 2.34 while the others were near 0.77. One of the network's seven outputs had "died" (see Findings).
  5. Fixed and re-ran. Added automatic re-initialisation for dead outputs and re-ran both neural models in 24 minutes. The baseline results were reused, since nothing about them changed.

Choices the paper didn't specify

  • Epochs: up to 50, keeping the best epoch on the 1% validation split (patience 5).
  • KNN-baseline is item-based. A user-based version would need a 72k × 72k similarity matrix, about 40 GB.
  • The surprise baselines train on the full 80%, since they need no validation split.

Reproducibility

  • Every seed is fixed (Python, NumPy, TensorFlow, fold shuffles), and TensorFlow runs in deterministic mode on CPU. Fold 1 gave identical numbers in two separate runs.
  • Results are saved per fold as each method finishes, so a killed run resumes where it stopped.
  • Test predictions are saved, so metrics can be recomputed with a different K without retraining.

7 · Findings

What the run showed

Finding 1: the paper's output layer can silently break the model

The paper puts a ReLU on the output layer. ReLU outputs max(0, x), so if one output's input drifts negative for every hotel and user, that output is stuck at 0 and its gradient is 0. It can never recover. When that happens to, say, the "Value" output, net 2 receives Value = 0 stars for every test row and the overall prediction collapses.

MAE of each of the 25 runs, before and after the fix
Each dot is one train/test run. Hover a dot for its fold and what died.
Our modelSingle DNNSVD (unaffected)

How often

Net 1 lost an output in 9 of 25 runs. One run needed 3 restarts. The Single DNN lost its only output in 2 runs. Net 2 never did. Before the fix, only 3 runs stood out clearly (MAE 2.34, 4.07, 4.07). The others were partial failures of 0.84–0.89 that looked plausible and quietly pulled the average up.

The fix

After training, if any output is 0 for every training row, the network is re-initialised with a new seed and retrained, up to 5 times. Every restart is logged and counted. The architecture is unchanged, and the same rule applies to the Single DNN so the comparison stays fair. With --max-restarts 0 you get the paper's original behaviour.

Finding 2: the criteria network learns everything in one epoch

Early stopping picked epoch 1 in all 25 runs. Since almost every user appears once, the user embeddings memorise their single training review almost immediately, and validation error rises from epoch 2. This is likely why our results vary more than the paper's (std 0.017 vs 0.005; runs range 0.74–0.82 with no outliers). It also explains why dead outputs are common: the weights kept are from very early in training, before a struggling output has had time to recover.

Finding 3: net 1 is the bottleneck

Fed the true criteria, net 2 predicts the overall rating with MAE 0.269. Given the real criteria, the overall star rating is very predictable. Nearly all of the chained model's 0.77 error comes from predicting the criteria for users the model has hardly seen.

Finding 4: MAP and FCP don't match the paper for any method

MAP ≈ MRR ≈ 0.777

Most test users have exactly one test hotel. With one item, the paper's MAP formula (eq. 19) reduces to MRR, and both equal "is that hotel relevant?", which doesn't depend on the model. So every method scores the same. The paper's much lower MAP (0.30–0.46) must come from a variant it doesn't describe.

FCP ≈ 0.66–0.69

Higher than the paper's ~0.51 for every method, and computed exactly as eq. 14–18 define it. SlopeOne scores 0.05 because it predicts the same value for unseen users, and the paper counts ties as wrong. The paper's 0.51 ± 0.066 suggests it used a different tie rule.

These differences apply equally to the baselines, whose MAE, F1 and F2 otherwise match the paper. That points to the paper's metric code rather than to anything in our model or data.
